Laurentia — the Precambrian craton at the core of North America and Greenland — was a major continent following its assembly 1.8 billion years ago. Its position is central to reconstructions of the Paleoproterozoic to Mesoproterozoic supercontinent Nuna and the Mesoproterozoic to Neoproterozoic supercontinent Rodinia. It has a rich record of well-dated paleomagnetic poles.

This site documents an update to the compilation of Laurentian paleomagnetic poles for the Precambrian presented and synthesized in [Swanson-Hysell (2021), "The Precambrian paleogeography of Laurentia"](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-818533-9.00009-6) and [Evans et al. (2021), "An expanding list of reliable paleomagnetic poles for Precambrian tectonic reconstructions"](https://doi.org/10.1016/B978-0-12-818533-9.00007-2). That work represents the culmination of Nordic Paleomagnetism Workshops leading up to a workshop in 2017 that lead both to compilation as well as community assessment of paleomagnetic poles. Subsequent workshops in Kringerdalen (Norway) in 2022 and Iloranta (Finland) in 2026 have synthesized advances over the past decade.

In the current work, each Laurentia pole is, where possible, recreated from the site level: site data are reassembled into [MagIC](https://earthref.org/MagIC) contributions, mean poles are recomputed, and reliability is evaluated against the [R-criteria of Meert et al. (2020)](resources/Meert2020_R_criteria.md). The goal is a transparent assessment of the pole database in conjunction with living documents that can continue to updated as new paleomagnetic and geochronology datasets are developed in the years ahead.

The most consequential changes in the new work are concentrated in the Mesoproterozoic and Neoproterozoic. New geochronology and paleomagnetic data have fundamentally changed our understanding of the transition from the Keweenawan Track to the Grenville loop from the late Stenian Period of the Mesoproterozoic into the early Tonian Period of the Neoproterozoic. These changes have major implications for both the position of the  Rodinia as well as efforts to use the record of Laurentia paleomagnetic poles to constraint the relative position of other continents within the supercontinent. The [revisions and additions](changes.md) page highlights the new poles since the Evans et al. (2021) compilation as well as changes (including deletations) that have been made to poles within the earlier compilation.
